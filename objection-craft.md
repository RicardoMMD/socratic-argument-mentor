# Objection craft

Read this at Phase 3. An objection either changes how someone thinks or it is decoration. The difference is almost always concreteness.

## The anatomy of an objection that lands

Three parts, in this order:

1. **The strongest version**, with something specific behind it.
2. **What survives** — the narrowed premise that would still be defensible.
3. **A question** they have to answer themselves.

Part 2 is what separates this from mere criticism. Without it you have taken something away and given nothing back, and the person's rational response is to defend rather than think. With it, you have handed them a better argument than the one they arrived with, and they can accept it without conceding anything.

## Sources of specificity, roughly in order of force

**Named positions.** A serious philosophical or theoretical stance that holds the opposite. "Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis treats the tool as part of the cognitive system, which makes your framing of delegation as loss a question-begging one." Naming a position converts "some people might disagree" into a live opponent.

**Empirical evidence with authors and dates.** "Sala and Gobet's meta-analyses on chess, music, and working-memory training found near-zero far transfer." Give the year. If you are unsure of the detail, say which part you are unsure of — "I believe this is Thorndike and Woodworth, around 1901, though check the date" costs you nothing and a fabrication costs everything.

**Historical precedent.** Every claim about a new technology degrading human capacity has predecessors: Socrates on writing in the *Phaedrus*, the printing press, the calculator, the search engine. The precedent does not refute the claim, but it does place a burden: what is different this time?

**Base rates and prior attempts.** If someone predicts a market, ask what happened to the last one that tried. The brain-training industry's trajectory — rapid growth, the 2014 Stanford Center on Longevity letter, the 2016 Lumosity settlement — is a real constraint on any prediction of a cognitive-fitness industry.

**Structural counterexamples.** A case that has the same form as their argument but an obviously wrong conclusion. This is the cleanest way to attack validity rather than truth.

**Distributional objections.** Their claim holds for them, or for the motivated, or for experts. Ask what fraction of the population the thesis needs, and whether the mechanism works for the median case or only the tail.

## Formulating "what survives"

The narrowed version usually comes from adding one of these:

- **A domain restriction.** True of specific skills, not general capacity.
- **A conditional.** True depending on the mode of use, not unconditionally.
- **A mechanism change.** True, but through a different causal route — which changes the implications.
- **An actor change.** True of institutions rather than individuals. Demand for certification is not the same as demand from people who noticed their own decline.
- **A temporal bound.** True now, for a reason that expires. Arbitrages close; skills the tool absorbs stop being scarce.

Say which of these you applied. The person learns the move, not just the result.

## Socratic questions that work

A good question has a genuine answer the person could produce, and producing it changes something. Test: could they answer "yes" or "no" and move on? Then it is not a good question.

**Load-bearing questions** — ask what the argument rests on: "if the narrow version were the only one available, would your prediction still stand?"

**Criterion questions** — ask them to define a term they are using as a measure: "you say the replacement work is less demanding. Demanding in what sense — effort, novelty, depth, difficulty of acquisition?"

**Discrimination questions** — ask for a test that separates two hypotheses: "what would you observe that distinguishes 'I write worse now' from 'I now compare myself against a higher standard'?"

**Own-behaviour questions** — when their conduct and their thesis diverge, ask about the gap. These are the sharpest questions available and should be used sparingly.

**Motive questions** — at the end, when the market and the evidence have both stopped supporting the thesis: "so why do you still want this?" Often reveals that the real claim is about values rather than facts, which is not a weakness but a different kind of argument.

## Asking for empirical information they have and you do not

When a premise turns on a number the person could actually know — their team's token spend as a share of a developer's cost, how often the failure they describe occurs — ask for it. This does two things: it is often decisive, and it teaches that the premise was empirical all along rather than something to be settled by argument.

## Proposing falsifiable tests

The best outcome of an objection is sometimes not a concession but an experiment. When a claim rests on introspection, design the test:

- Specify what to do, in enough detail to actually be done.
- Specify the **comparison**, and check that the baseline is the right one. Comparing a current draft against what a machine would produce is not a test of decline; comparing it against something they wrote four years ago is.
- Specify what each outcome would mean, in advance.

A good test is worth more than winning the round, and it is the only thing in this protocol that produces new evidence rather than rearranging existing evidence.

## Calibrating severity

Order objections by how much of the conclusion collapses if they succeed — not by how confident you are, and not by how clever the objection is. An interesting objection to a peripheral premise wastes a round. Say explicitly which premise the whole argument hangs on; that sentence alone is often the most useful thing in Phase 2.

## Where to grant

Grant early and grant specifically. When a premise resists, say so and explain the mechanism of its resistance. When their defense refutes part of your objection, retract that part in the same words you used to assert it, and say what remains standing. An examiner who has never conceded has no credibility when they press, and by round four the person will have stopped listening.
