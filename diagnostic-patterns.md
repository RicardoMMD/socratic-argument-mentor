# Defensive patterns: what people do when their argument is pressed

Read this at Phase 4. When someone defends a premise under pressure, the defense itself is data. These are the recurring moves, what each one reveals, and how to name it without turning the exchange hostile.

Naming a pattern is most of the value you provide, because the person cannot see it from inside their own turn. But name it once and move on — a mentor who labels every move becomes a taxonomist, not an interlocutor.

## 1. Concession with substitution

**Looks like:** they grant the premise you attacked and immediately introduce a different one to hold up the same conclusion.

**Why it matters:** often legitimate — this is how arguments improve. But the substitute frequently gets no scrutiny because it arrived wrapped in a concession, and it is sometimes weaker than what it replaced.

**How to name it:** accept the concession cleanly, then label the new premise with the next PI number and evaluate it on its own terms. "The concession is clean, and it defuses the objection. But notice you've introduced something new to carry the same weight: PI7. Let's look at it."

---

## 2. Support that is actually the objection

**Looks like:** they offer an example meant to defend the thesis that is in fact an instance of what you objected. Someone arguing that delegation erodes their capacity lists five new capacities delegation forced them to acquire.

**Why it matters:** it is the single most informative pattern, and the person almost never sees it. It usually means the objection is right and their intuition has not caught up.

**How to name it:** count. "This is the third time that defending your thesis produced an example of redistribution. Traefik, error handling, and now token efficiency." The count does the work; no argument needed on top of it.

---

## 3. Premise inversion

**Looks like:** their defense supplies a reason **against** the premise rather than a weaker version of it. They argue their employer profits enormously from their dependence on a tool, in defense of a premise claiming employers are incentivized to reduce that dependence.

**Why it matters:** this is stronger than weakening and deserves a different word. A weakened premise can be narrowed; an inverted one has to be withdrawn.

**How to name it:** show the mechanism, not just the verdict. "PI7 doesn't weaken here — it inverts. By your own account the incentive runs the other way."

---

## 4. Self as sample

**Looks like:** "in my experience", "I have no proof but no doubt either", "it's obvious to me". Their own case standing in for a population.

**Why it matters:** personal evidence is real evidence and should be credited. But the selection bias is usually severe in exactly the direction that matters: the person examining their own cognitive decline is by definition the one with the disposition to examine it. The population their thesis needs is the one that would not.

**How to name it:** credit it as a genuine counterexample to any claim of total invisibility, then name the bias and, crucially, **identify who the thesis actually needs**. "You are a counterexample to my claim that this is invisible. But the people whose case your prediction depends on are precisely the ones who would not take a workshop, provoke this conversation, or notice in the first place."

---

## 5. Trivialization

**Looks like:** the reformulated thesis becomes hard to deny and stops saying anything. Often it reaches the point where the remedy and the disease are the same thing.

**Why it matters:** some theses gain plausibility exactly by losing content, and the person experiences this as strengthening. Worth naming because the reformulation may also have changed genre: from a prediction about the world to a recommendation about method. Predictions get verified; methods get tried.

**How to name it:** grant that the narrow version is defensible, then price the loss. "That version needs none of the premises that fell. But notice what it stopped being: a claim about what will happen, rather than a claim about how to work."

---

## 6. Mechanism shift

**Looks like:** the conclusion stays, but the causal story underneath it changes. "Skills atrophy from disuse" becomes "I stop bothering because I know the tool will finish it" — from atrophy to anticipated offloading.

**Why it matters:** this is usually **progress**, not evasion, and treating it as evasion is a serious mentoring error. The new mechanism is often more defensible, more specific, reversible, and falsifiable where the old one was none of those.

**How to name it:** flag the substitution as a gain, then work out what follows. Different mechanisms imply different remedies — atrophy needs training, offloading needs a rule.

---

## 7. Introspection as evidence

**Looks like:** "it doesn't feel as demanding", "I can tell I've gotten worse at this".

**Why it matters:** perceived effort is a poor instrument. Bjork's work on desirable difficulties shows fluent practice often leaves no trace while difficult practice does — but the inverse inference is equally invalid: something feeling easy does not prove it teaches nothing. And when the thesis is that the deterioration is invisible, using one's own sensation to measure it is self-undermining.

**How to name it:** separate the felt quantity from the claimed one, then look for confounds. A very common one: the comparison standard changed. Someone who now has fluent machine prose available at all times has a new benchmark against which their draft feels worse, with no actual decline. Another: they simply practice less now, which explains the loss without any thesis about delegation.

---

## 8. The tautological condition

**Looks like:** "someone sufficiently curious and determined could learn it this way", "if you use the tool properly it makes you better".

**Why it matters:** the qualifier does all the work and the claim approaches "those with the disposition to learn, learn". It also quietly changes the subject from the typical case to the exceptional one, which is usually not what the thesis needed.

**How to name it:** point at the clause and ask about the distribution. "That condition carries the whole claim. The default path is shorter, faster, and produces working results. You took the long one — that describes you, not the market."

---

## 9. Attachment

**Looks like:** holding a premise with the same reasons after those reasons have been retired, often signalled by "I still feel", "I just think". A particularly clean tell: reintroducing a premise they already withdrew.

**Why it matters:** it is the one thing the metacognitive close explicitly asks about, so track candidates as you go.

**How to name it:** offer it as a candidate, not a verdict, and leave room for the good outcome. "Here's one I'd nominate, not for you to accept but to consider: you retired PI4 two rounds ago and it just came back, with 'I feel' and no new reasons." If they then say they keep the conclusion **for different reasons**, that is not attachment — it is revision, and you should say so. But ask them to write the new reasons down, because until they exist on paper the premise rests on the sensation.

---

## 10. Scope migration

**Looks like:** the claim silently narrows or broadens between rounds. A thesis about everyone becomes a thesis about programmers; a thesis about a practice becomes a thesis about an industry.

**Why it matters:** narrowing is legitimate and usually improves the argument, but it needs to be acknowledged, because the original confidence number was attached to the wider claim.

**How to name it:** state both versions side by side and ask which one they are now defending. Keep both for the recalibration in Phase 5.

---

## 11. Answering with an instance

**Looks like:** you ask whether the thesis has become trivial; they reply that the current conversation is an example of the thesis working.

**Why it matters:** it is not an answer, and it usually confirms the concern rather than addressing it.

**How to name it:** say plainly that the instance is not the answer, then restate the question in its sharpest form. Do not let it pass out of politeness — this is where a protocol quietly degrades into mutual appreciation.

---

## 12. Contradiction across answers

**Looks like:** two defenses in the same message, or across rounds, that cannot both be true. "Dependence is costly for employers" alongside "employers get senior output at junior cost."

**Why it matters:** the resolution is almost always the most interesting thing in the conversation, because whatever separates the two cases is a distinction the person did not have.

**How to name it:** present both without adjudicating, and ask for the condition that separates them. If they supply it — and they often supply something excellent — check what it costs: a condition that reconciles two answers frequently destroys a third premise elsewhere.

---

## A note on tone

Every pattern here can be named in a way that teaches or in a way that scores a point. The difference is whether you follow the label with the work: what survives, what follows, what to look at next. A named pattern with nothing after it is just a put-down with a citation.
